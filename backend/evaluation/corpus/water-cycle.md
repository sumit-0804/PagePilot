# The Water Cycle

## Overview

The water cycle, also called the hydrological cycle, is the continuous movement of water between the oceans, the atmosphere and the land. It is driven mainly by energy from the Sun and by gravity. The total amount of water on Earth stays roughly the same; the cycle only moves it between stores and changes its state between liquid, gas and solid.

## Evaporation and transpiration

Evaporation turns liquid water into water vapour, mostly from the surface of the oceans, which supply about 86 percent of global evaporation. Plants also release water vapour through small pores in their leaves called stomata, a process known as transpiration. Together, evaporation and transpiration are called evapotranspiration. Sublimation is the change of ice or snow directly into water vapour without melting first.

## Condensation and clouds

As moist air rises, it expands and cools. When it cools to its dew point, water vapour condenses onto tiny particles of dust, salt or smoke called condensation nuclei, forming the droplets that make up clouds. Fog is simply a cloud that forms at ground level.

## Precipitation

Precipitation is any water that falls from clouds to the ground, including rain, snow, sleet and hail. It happens when cloud droplets or ice crystals grow too heavy to stay in the air. Hail forms when strong updrafts carry raindrops high into freezing parts of a thunderstorm cloud again and again, adding layers of ice each time.

## Water on and under the land

When rain reaches the ground, some of it flows over the surface as runoff into streams, rivers and eventually the sea. Some soaks into the soil through infiltration and slowly moves down to become groundwater. Groundwater is stored in layers of permeable rock or sediment called aquifers, and it can stay underground for thousands of years before it returns to the surface through springs or wells.

## Stores of water

About 97 percent of Earth's water is salt water in the oceans. Of the freshwater that remains, most is locked up in glaciers and ice caps, and much of the rest is groundwater, so only a small fraction is found in lakes and rivers. The time water spends in each store is called its residence time; a water molecule may stay in the atmosphere for only about nine days.
